# Parent–Child Joint Counselling Process Template

This document is an applied, role-general companion to the relational shared-state formalism. It is written for a counsellor, mediator, clinician, caregiver meeting, or supported family discussion. Replace **Parent**, **Child**, and the bracketed project details with the relevant roles and facts.

It does not diagnose either participant, determine disputed facts, or imply that every defensive interaction is deliberate manipulation. Its purpose is to preserve the distinctions needed for a fair reconstruction and a workable repair process.

## 1. Presenting purpose

The Child wants help describing a recurring communication and relational pattern using a recent household project as one concrete example.

The issue is not fundamentally the project itself. It concerns whether each person’s contribution can reliably affect the shared conversational state:

- whether a contribution remains part of the discussion long enough to be answered;
- whether questions, preferences, invitations, intentions, commitments, and agreements retain distinct meanings;
- whether decision ownership is recorded accurately;
- whether limited future capacity is protected;
- whether conflict can be repaired without the initiating issue being displaced by the Parent’s or Child’s immediate emotional response.

## 2. Concrete entry-point incident

Use a recent bounded example, such as a garden purchase, household repair, appointment, move, application, or shared errand.

Record separately:

1. what was discussed beforehand;
2. what each participant actually said;
3. what was purchased, decided, submitted, or begun;
4. what remained conditional or unresolved;
5. what later work or consequences followed;
6. who was expected to carry that work;
7. what objection was raised;
8. what allegation or counter-allegation followed;
9. whether the original issue was answered.

A useful incident form is:

> Before the activity, the Parent asked for or received the Child’s input. The Child understood that certain constraints or preferences had been discussed. The Parent then acted in a way that appeared inconsistent with those constraints. The Child asked practical questions. The Parent experienced the questions as criticism or accusation. The discussion shifted from the project discrepancy to the effect of the Child’s objection. The initiating discrepancy and the later allegation both remained unresolved.

This form must be corrected wherever the facts differ.

## 3. The central shared-state problem

The Child’s concern may be more precise than “the Parent does not listen.” It may be that the Child’s contribution does not reliably constrain what happens next.

A recurring sequence can be tested:

1. The Child introduces a story, question, preference, remembered agreement, or concern.
2. Something in it triggers an association, solution, preference, defensive feeling, or regulatory need for the Parent.
3. The Parent’s present response becomes the organising subject.
4. The Child’s unfinished contribution stops constraining the interaction.
5. The Child attempts to restore the original thread or clarify what was said.
6. The restoration attempt is experienced as criticism, pedantry, accusation, aggression, or invalidation.
7. The Child becomes distressed, over-explains, shuts down, or withdraws.
8. The withdrawal is interpreted without preserving its causal connection to the interruption or displacement.
9. The withdrawal, distance, or tone becomes the new problem requiring reassurance, apology, or reconciliation.

The counsellor should test each link rather than assuming the whole sequence.

## 4. Misattunement does not imply absence of care

The Parent may provide substantial care, practical support, advice, organisation, transport, money, housing, advocacy, or companionship. The Child may recognise and value this.

The issue can still be one of fragmented or poorly timed attention. Relevant questions include:

- Can the Child finish a story or explanation?
- Does an answer alter the decision procedure?
- Does an apparent agreement retain the same meaning later?
- Is a practical question treated as a question or as an attack?
- Is withdrawal recognised as a rupture signal?
- Does an unresolved concern remain open?
- Is a later desire for harmony treated as though repair has already occurred?

ADHD, stress, grief, trauma, fatigue, pain, executive dysfunction, or caregiver strain may contribute to interruption, rapid association, impulsive problem-solving, or lost conversational threads. Mechanism is relevant to repair, but explanation does not by itself settle consent, provenance, impact, or accountability.

## 5. Responses attach to exact propositions

The object of response is often conditional and nested rather than a simple yes-or-no statement.

For example:

> If the Parent is genuinely unable to do the task, the matter is urgent, the Child has enough capacity, no less burdensome option exists, and this concerns one bounded occasion, then the Child may consider helping.

This is not equivalent to:

> The Child agrees to perform this class of task whenever it arises.

Keep distinct:

- hearing an option;
- leaving it open;
- considering it;
- preferring it;
- intending it;
- committing to it;
- committing to one bounded instance;
- accepting an ongoing role.

The core rule is:

> An affirmation applies to the exact proposal, conditions, scope, decision episode, and time that were actually affirmed.

It does not automatically propagate to descendant tasks, broader circumstances, stronger modalities, later episodes, or changed capacity states.

## 6. Consultation, consent, and decision provenance

Keep separate:

- discussion;
- request for an opinion;
- expression of preference;
- consideration of an option;
- proposal of a plan;
- unilateral decision;
- intention;
- commitment;
- mutual agreement.

The following implications are invalid without further evidence:

- asked does not imply agreed;
- discussed does not imply jointly decided;
- silence does not imply assent;
- accompanying someone does not imply accepting all downstream work;
- participation does not imply ownership of every consequence.

When a response is absent and action is necessary, a fair provenance statement is:

> “No response was recorded, so the Parent made a unilateral decision.”

It should not later become:

> “We agreed.”

## 7. Future-capacity capture

Ambiguous present participation can later be reconstructed as a demand against future labour.

The sequence is:

> invitation now → attributed commitment later → demand on future capacity.

This is especially serious when the Child’s physical, executive, emotional, financial, or communicative capacity is limited or unpredictable.

The protective rule is:

> A future obligation requires an explicit commitment to that obligation.

Practical planning should therefore record:

- who owns the project;
- who owns each descendant task;
- what happens if capacity changes;
- what counts as a new request;
- whether the task expires;
- how a handover occurs;
- what closure duties remain.

## 8. Process-bearing commitments

Some decisions open extended processes rather than single acts. A process may accumulate:

- purchases;
- unfinished physical tasks;
- applications and documents;
- appointments;
- contacts and queue positions;
- external dependencies;
- expiring opportunities;
- assumptions about who will complete it.

Therefore:

> “The outcome is unfinished” does not mean “nothing happened.”

And:

> “Stop participating” does not automatically erase completed work, pending external processes, closure requirements, search capital, or the need for a handover.

After authority or cooperation changes, distinguish:

- already completed acts;
- externally pending processes;
- required closure;
- preservation of existing work;
- handover operations;
- optional continuation;
- genuinely new discretionary expansion.

## 9. More options are not always better

Each live option may require:

- remembering it;
- monitoring it;
- gathering evidence;
- responding before expiry;
- completing physical work;
- maintaining documents;
- managing consequences;
- tolerating uncertainty.

Additional branches may worsen the outcome when they:

- exceed servicing capacity;
- duplicate the same blocked basin;
- divert effort from the desired attractor;
- provide immediate relief but create a long-term trap;
- conflict with other live branches;
- require incompatible representations or commitments.

The practical question is not:

> “How many possibilities can remain open?”

It is:

> “Which possibilities are genuinely feasible, jointly serviceable, compatible with one another, informative, and likely to move the system toward the desired outcome?”

## 10. Feelings and behavioural allegations

Feelings are real reports of experience. They are not automatically complete findings about another person’s behaviour, intention, or history.

Keep distinct:

> “That question felt accusatory to me.”

and:

> “You gaslit me.”

The first reports an experience. The second alleges identifiable conduct.

A serious behavioural allegation should identify:

- actor;
- statement or act;
- context;
- affected person;
- alleged effect;
- why the conduct fits the label;
- available evidence or receipt.

The standard applies equally to Parent and Child. Neither participant should rely on a global label where the relevant conduct can be particularised.

Acknowledging emotional impact does not by itself concede the behavioural characterisation:

> impact acknowledged ≠ allegation admitted.

## 11. Preserve causal order

Conflict reconstruction should begin immediately before the objection.

A possible sequence is:

1. The Parent acts in a way that affects the Child.
2. The Child questions, objects to, or names the conduct.
3. The Parent feels criticised, accused, ashamed, frightened, or hurt.
4. The discussion shifts to the Child’s tone, persistence, or alleged misconduct.
5. The Child asks for clarification or attempts to return to the initiating event.
6. The resolution attempt is experienced as further aggression or invalidation.
7. The Parent becomes the injured party and the Child becomes the alleged offender.
8. The initiating conduct remains unresolved.

This may resemble a DARVO-like role reversal, but the label should remain provisional and structural. It does not establish deliberate strategy, motive, or the truth of the initiating complaint.

Both questions must remain live:

- What happened before the objection?
- Was the objection communicated unfairly or harmfully?

The second must not replace the first.

## 12. Requests for clarification can become recursive conflict

A request for particulars attempts to turn an undefined moral accusation into something answerable:

- Which exact words?
- Which act?
- In what context?
- What effect?
- What change is requested?

The request itself may then be characterised as invalidation, control, refusal to recognise feelings, or “going in circles.”

The recursion is:

1. original issue remains unresolved;
2. counter-allegation is introduced;
3. request to clarify the allegation becomes another allegation;
4. the number of unresolved issues increases.

A counselling process should stop this expansion by typing and resolving one node at a time.

## 13. Reconnection is not repair

After conflict, one participant may return quickly to cheerfulness, playfulness, ordinary conversation, or practical activity. This may be self-soothing, avoidance, atmosphere regulation, or a sincere bid for reconnection.

It should not silently update the shared state to “resolved.”

A problematic sequence is:

> conflict → no resolution → surface normality → the person still distressed becomes the apparent source of tension.

Reconnection can be welcomed while rupture status remains open.

Repair requires at least:

1. identifying what happened;
2. preserving causal order;
3. recognising each person’s experience;
4. clarifying disputed facts and allegations;
5. identifying the effect;
6. agreeing on a changed future procedure;
7. checking whether the change occurred.

## 14. Repeating repair cycle

A recurring cycle may be:

1. Parent behaviour affects the Child.
2. Child reacts, objects, or protects themself.
3. Focus shifts to the effect of the objection on the Parent.
4. Child tries to recover the initiating issue.
5. Child becomes overwhelmed and seeks space.
6. Parent becomes anxious, guilty, or afraid of disconnection.
7. Parent seeks apology, reassurance, or rapid closeness.
8. Emotional security returns without sufficient behavioural analysis.
9. A similar incident recurs.
10. The new incident carries accumulated exhaustion from the earlier cycles.

The Child’s need for space should be examined as possible self-protection, not presumed punishment. The Parent’s desire for reconnection should be recognised without treating it as proof that repair is complete.

## 15. Memory and factual continuity

Complex interactions are often compressed into labels such as:

- “We agreed.”
- “You rejected it.”
- “You did not help.”
- “You were aggressive.”
- “Nothing happened.”
- “It was resolved.”

These labels may erase that:

- an option remained open but was never selected;
- a task expired before it could be completed;
- a process was active without a terminal outcome;
- participation occurred without acceptance of every consequence;
- emotional impact was acknowledged without admitting the alleged behaviour;
- reconnection occurred without repair.

The shared record should preserve the path, not merely the terminal label.

## 16. Care, dependency, and moral authority

Practical care can be substantial and burdensome. Caregiver strain should be discussable directly.

Care must nevertheless remain separate from the merits of a specific complaint.

Avoid the moral substitution:

> Parent provides care, therefore Parent is the generous or credible party.
>
> Child receives care, therefore Child’s criticism is ingratitude.

A person can appreciate care while objecting to interruption, unsupported allegations, inaccurate decision provenance, imposed obligations, or failed repair.

Dependency increases practical vulnerability. It should not create moral subordination or reduce the dependent person’s entitlement to consent, memory, boundaries, and factual review.

Present care also does not automatically erase the history that contributed to present support needs.

## 17. Wider family and institutional pattern

The same structure may appear across generations or institutions:

- a difficult event is acknowledged verbally;
- the available alternatives are reconstructed as though they were safely selectable;
- the person seeking continuity becomes “obsessive,” “aggressive,” or “unstable”;
- surface normality is restored;
- the underlying issue remains uncorrected.

This is a hypothesis to investigate, not a conclusion to impose. Each current person and incident must remain individually evaluated.

## 18. No-win structures

Test whether the Child experiences a double bind such as:

- practical questions are accusatory;
- naming a pattern is abusive;
- asking what an allegation refers to invalidates feelings;
- continuing to seek an answer is circular;
- distress is retaliation;
- explanation is overwhelming or controlling;
- space triggers pressure for rapid reconciliation;
- remaining upset maintains the tension;
- reconnecting makes the initiating issue disappear;
- dependency turns objection into ingratitude.

The existence of a felt double bind does not prove every interpretation correct. It signals that the process may lack a safe, recognised route for clarification, refusal, pause, or correction.

## 19. Counselling goals

A minimally corrigible shared process should establish that:

- the original subject remains available until answered, explicitly deferred, or withdrawn by its originator;
- feelings do not automatically become findings about motive, conduct, agreement, or history;
- serious labels are connected to particulars;
- the standard applies equally to both participants;
- causal order is preserved;
- discussion, openness, preference, intention, commitment, unilateral decision, and agreement remain distinct;
- conditional statements retain their conditions;
- parent-level activities do not silently propagate into descendant tasks;
- silence, shutdown, exhaustion, or failure to object repeatedly are not assent;
- unilateral decisions are recorded as unilateral;
- future capacity is not captured by ambiguity;
- added branches are assessed for feasibility, capacity, compatibility, and attractor value;
- either person can pause without erasing the issue;
- reconnection does not substitute for repair;
- caregiver strain can be discussed without becoming evidence against the dependent person’s credibility;
- previous versions and changed accounts retain provenance;
- correction does not trigger retaliation.

## 20. Central counselling questions

A central process question is:

> How can this relationship preserve a shared conversational and relational reality when one participant’s immediate association, preference, defensiveness, guilt, urgency, or desire for harmony displaces the original subject—and when the other participant’s attempt to restore continuity is then experienced as the problem?

A central decision question is:

> How can decisions preserve the exact conditions, scope, ownership, timing, and future consequences of what each person actually agreed to, rather than allowing participation, silence, or ambiguity to become an unlimited future obligation?

A central repair question is:

> How can both the initiating event and the manner of objection remain examinable, so that neither care nor hurt feelings erase factual continuity and neither factual review erases emotional experience?

## Purpose

This note isolates the three legal questions that now matter most after acquisition of the 9281 negotiated approval and plans. It is written for counsel and community use. It is not legal advice.

## 1. Nature Conservation Act ss 102–107 — interim conservation order

### What the law requires

Section 102 does not require a perfect map overlap between a clearing polygon and an animal or habitat point.

The key question is whether threatened or near-threatened wildlife, critical habitat, an area of major interest or a protected area is subject to a threatening process that is likely to have a significant detrimental effect.

Section 103 allows an order to relate to land even where the wildlife or habitat is not physically within that land.

### What already supports the Woogaroo case

- Koala is listed as Endangered in Queensland.
- `9281/2024/OW` authorises a concrete process involving vegetation clearing, earthworks and stormwater works.
- `A12705838` gives the negotiated approved plan-scale works and clearing surfaces.
- The project’s own ecology records Koala scats, food trees, habitat score 7/10 and a connected habitat landscape greater than 500 ha.
- The project’s own ecology records about 136 ha of direct Koala habitat clearing and about 26 ha of indirect impact.
- The project consultant concluded that clearing and functional loss of about 136 ha would be a significant impact on Koala habitat critical to survival under the federal guideline then used.
- The same project material records a fragmentation pathway: surrounding development is expected to reduce movement opportunities and further fragment habitat.

These points make the s 102 route serious enough for expert and counsel assessment. They do not yet prove that the Minister must or will make an order.

### The strongest points against the case

- Saunders Havill assigned the site Koala recovery value 0 and argued that urban barriers and isolation reduced its population-level recovery importance.
- The local approval contains mitigation and management conditions, including retained-tree protection, arborist involvement, fauna spotter-catcher requirements, pre-clearance planning and erosion/sediment controls.
- The final 2026 federal proposal may differ from the 2019 ecological baseline.

These points should be tested, not hidden.

### What remains open

The missing legal/ecological bridge is:

**Is the approved vegetation-clearing and earthworks process likely to have a significant detrimental effect on qualifying threatened wildlife or habitat?**

The best next evidence is a short ecological opinion that addresses the actual Queensland wording and explains:

- the qualifying wildlife or habitat;
- the threatening process;
- the causal pathway;
- likely magnitude and duration of harm;
- fragmentation and connectivity effects;
- reversibility or irreversibility;
- whether the approval conditions materially reduce the likely effect.

In parallel, obtain Condition 6(a), prestart and commencement records because they affect urgency.

## 2. Nature Conservation Act s 13 — critical habitat

### What the law requires

Section 13 defines critical habitat as habitat essential for conservation of a viable population of protected wildlife or a community of native wildlife. The habitat can qualify even if the wildlife is not presently occupying every part of it.

The question is therefore stronger than “is this good habitat?” or “are Koalas present?”.

### What already supports the case

The same Springview project material records:

- remnant/native vegetation;
- recognised Koala food trees;
- Koala scats;
- Woogaroo/Opossum Creek connectivity;
- habitat-connectivity score 2;
- a mapped connected habitat area greater than 500 ha;
- habitat score 7/10;
- substantial proposed habitat loss; and
- expected further fragmentation from surrounding development.

This strongly supports habitat function and conservation importance.

### The strongest point against the case

Saunders Havill’s recovery-value-0 conclusion is the main adverse proposition presently identified. It argues that roads and urbanisation make the site relatively isolated and not viable to support a Koala population.

That cannot simply be ignored. But it also should not automatically decide the s 13 question because the old federal recovery-value construct is not the same wording as Queensland s 13.

### What remains open

Two bridges still need to be proved:

1. **Which viable Koala population or wildlife community is the relevant one?**
2. **Why is this habitat essential to conservation of that population/community?**

The most useful expert work would therefore ask:

- what happens to movement, persistence, breeding/dispersal and resource access if this habitat is removed or severed;
- whether Opossum/Woogaroo connectivity is redundant or a bottleneck;
- whether nearby habitat can perform the same function now;
- whether later development since 2019 makes the remaining connected habitat more important;
- whether the factual basis of the 2019 recovery-value-0 conclusion still holds.

Until those questions are answered, the s 13 case is strong on habitat function but not yet complete on statutory essentiality.

## 3. EPBC Act s 43B — historical clearing / continuation of use

### What changed

From 1 December 2025, the EPBC Act continuation-of-use exemption in s 43B cannot be relied on for vegetation clearing where the land has not been cleared for at least 15 years, unless the forestry-operation exception applies.

This is a separate question from whether EPBC 2019/8575 should be approved or refused under Part 9.

### Current Woogaroo position

We have not located a project or agency document showing that the proponent is presently relying on s 43B for the 2019/8575 action.

That means s 43B should be treated as a **conditional exemption-audit issue**, not the main merits argument.

It becomes important if a party says that a present clearing action does not require federal approval because it is merely a lawful continuation of pre-EPBC land use.

### What would then have to be checked

For the exact clearing area in question:

- identify the use of the land immediately before commencement of the EPBC Act;
- show whether the present action is genuinely a continuation of that use;
- reconstruct whether the land has been cleared at any time during the preceding 15 years;
- determine whether the action is a forestry operation;
- check whether a specific environmental authorisation points instead to s 43A;
- keep the significant-impact question separate.

Historical aerials, woody-cover change, LiDAR and mature-forest evidence can help, but none alone proves the legal clearing history.

### Practical conclusion

Do the historical-clearing work now only to the extent that it is cheap and useful for other purposes. Escalate it into a full s 43B case only if an exemption theory is actually relied upon.

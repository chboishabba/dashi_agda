It separates (1) the Commonwealth's own offset-policy tests from (2) official landscape/vegetation context, (3) public-submission criticisms of the Springview offset proposal, (4) DASHI cross-source inferences and (5) the still-missing primary proponent offset package.

## Attribution discipline

All propositions in this note must retain their canonical provenance stage:

```text
external source claim
secondary source interpretation
repository reconstruction
cross-source inference

```

The stage is part of the claim. It must not be erased in downstream briefs.

In particular:

- Commonwealth offset-policy criteria are **external source claims** attributable to the Australian Government policy/guidance, not to the proponent and not to a court;
- Queensland vegetation/habitat/protected-estate material is **external source material** supporting general or mapped ecological/planning propositions, not a finding about the adequacy of the 2019/8575 offset;
- allegations about distant offset sites, ecological mismatch, monitoring gaps or local genetics from a public submission remain **secondary-source/submission propositions** unless independently source-paid;
- comparisons such as "high-pressure mature connected Woogaroo habitat may not be equivalent to lower-risk planted habitat elsewhere" are **DASHI cross-source inferences** unless a single external source expressly makes that project-specific proposition;
- none of these stages is a counsel opinion, agency finding, adjudicated fact or legal conclusion merely because the source is authoritative.

Therefore:

```text
attribution != truth
attribution != legal authority
attribution != applicability
submission != project fact
policy criterion != agency finding
general ecology != same-parcel fact
cross-source inference != source-author statement
```

## Bottom line

The public record already identifies a serious offset-law/evidence attack surface, but we should **not yet say the proposed offset has been proved inadequate**.

The Commonwealth's 2012 EPBC Environmental Offsets Policy says, among other things, that offsets:

- compensate only the residual impact left after avoidance and mitigation;
- must correlate to the same protected matter and impacted attribute;
- should generally be as close to the impact site as possible unless a more distant site produces a greater conservation benefit;
- must account for time to ecological gain and confidence/risk;
- must be additional to existing legal/planning/program obligations;
- must be measurable, monitorable, auditable and enforceable;
- must be scientifically robust and precautionary under uncertainty;
- for threatened-species/community habitat, must at least meet impact-site habitat quality or be managed to reach that quality over a defined period;
- must be proportionate in size/scale to the residual impact and the protected matter's status.

A detailed public submission responding to 2019/8575 alleges that the proposed offsets are almost 100 km away, that two are outside the local government area, that regional ecosystem/geology/landscape attributes differ, that one proposed property is in the Mt Walker West/Mt Mort area, that mature hollow-bearing ironbark/ecosystem features cannot be recreated quickly, that MNES baseline/monitoring is inadequate, and that local koala genetics/population structure may not be represented by distant sites.

Those are **submission propositions**, not agency findings. They become legally useful only when tested against the actual proponent offset package and the Commonwealth policy criteria.

## Highest-value tests for counsel/ecologists

### 1. Avoidance and mitigation first

Before asking whether an offset is good enough, identify what impacts remain **after** all feasible avoidance and mitigation.

```text
impact before avoidance
  !=
residual impact eligible for offsetting
```

The primary PD should show alternatives, avoided clearing, retained habitat, mitigation and the final residual impact.

### 2. Same protected matter and same impacted attribute

For each controlled matter, build:

```text
impact attribute
  ->
offset attribute claimed to compensate it
```

Examples include koala foraging/shelter/movement habitat, Grey-headed Flying-fox foraging resources, listed flora habitat, and Lowland Rainforest TEC attributes.

A large number of hectares somewhere else is not itself enough:

```text
large offset area != equivalent ecological function
```

### 3. Distance and local ecological function

The Commonwealth policy does not make distance an automatic invalidator. It says offsets should generally be as close as possible, but a more distant offset can be accepted if a greater conservation benefit is demonstrated.

So the exact counsel question is:

> What greater conservation benefit is said to justify the proposed distant sites, and is that proposition supported for the same protected matter/attribute affected at Woogaroo?

Distance matters most when it proxies a change in ecological function: a remote parcel may not replace local movement, dispersal, refuge or population-connectivity functions even if it supports the same species.

### 4. Existing mature/remnant forest versus planted or young regrowth habitat

This should now be treated as a first-class offset-quality issue rather than a rhetorical one.

Commonwealth offset guidance evaluates current habitat quality, future quality, time until ecological benefit and confidence in that future condition. Queensland vegetation guidance likewise distinguishes mature good-quality regrowth from degraded regrowth and fresh revegetation because successional stage affects ecological value, management burden and risk of failure.

Existing mature forest can provide immediately:

- established canopy and food-tree structure;
- large/mature trees;
- shelter and microhabitat;
- fallen timber/litter/understorey complexity;
- established movement pathways and canopy continuity;
- species interactions and soil/ecosystem structure that planted vegetation does not instantly reproduce.

For some species, tree size and age are themselves relevant habitat features; Queensland wildlife guidance explicitly notes that some animals require trees/shrubs of particular size or age, and that hollows, fallen timber and mature structural features are distinct habitat resources.

Therefore:

```text
planted hectares
  !=
existing mature-forest function
```

and:

```text
young regrowth
  !=
mature remnant habitat
```

without a species-specific quality/time/confidence bridge.

For each impact and offset site, counsel/ecologists should ask for:

- remnant / high-value-regrowth / planted status;
- vegetation age or successional stage;
- canopy height/closure and mature-tree abundance;
- food-tree composition;
- hollows, standing dead trees and fallen timber where relevant;
- understorey and floristic composition;
- evidence of actual use by the protected matter;
- years required to reach equivalent ecological function, not merely tree cover.

### 5. Development pressure and the counterfactual risk of loss

This is particularly important to the **additionality** side of the offset calculation.

The Commonwealth Offset Assessment Guide defines `risk of loss` as the chance that habitat at the offset site would otherwise be permanently lost in the foreseeable future. A large part of the conservation gain from protecting a site can therefore depend on the counterfactual:

> What would happen to this offset site if it were **not** used as an offset?

A parcel under genuine urban-development/clearing pressure may generate substantial risk-reduction credit when secured. Conversely, a remote parcel already unlikely to be cleared can have a low without-offset risk of loss; simply putting another protection instrument over it may generate comparatively little additional conservation gain.

Accordingly:

```text
large protected parcel
  !=
large additional conservation gain
```

and:

```text
low development pressure
  !=
high risk-of-loss credit
```

For the impact and offset sites, compare:

- zoning / PDA / structure-plan status;
- nearby approved or proposed development;
- road and infrastructure expansion;
- historical clearing rates;
- active development applications;
- tenure and existing statutory clearing restrictions;
- existing conservation covenants or government ownership;
- realistic lawful clearing pathways.

Do **not** assign a numeric probability of development without a defensible planning/economic model. The useful legal question is whether the proponent's assumed risk-of-loss values are reasonable and evidenced.

### 6. Proximity to existing protected estates and conservation network position

The impact site's landscape position may be unusually important.

Ipswich City Council describes **White Rock–Spring Mountain Conservation Estate** as more than 2,500 hectares, a core habitat area within the Flinders–Karawatha Corridor, supporting high conservation values and the headwaters of seven waterways. Council separately says **Ric Nattrass Environmental Park**, adjoining Woogaroo Creek, provides connectivity from White Rock–Spring Mountain to the Brisbane River corridor. The historic Springfield Structure Plan itself describes Springfield's environmental role as providing a habitat connection between major core habitat areas and linking to the proposed White Rock/Spring Mountain conservation area.

That does **not** yet prove the exact Springview action polygon forms the critical connecting segment. But it means proximity/connectivity to existing protected land is not an invented litigation theory; it is embedded in official local conservation planning.

For the impact site, measure:

- exact distance and functional connection to White Rock–Spring Mountain;
- relationship to Woogaroo Creek and Ric Nattrass Environmental Park;
- relationship to other secured habitat/corridor land;
- whether clearing narrows, severs or isolates a protected-area network.

For each offset site, independently ask:

- is it adjacent to existing protected land and therefore ecologically valuable?
- or is it already so constrained/protected that the **additionality / risk-of-loss gain** from the offset is small?

Those are different questions:

```text
adjacent to protected land
  -> may increase connectivity value
```

but also:

```text
already effectively protected
  -> may reduce additionality / avoided-loss credit
```

The offset assessment must not collapse them.

### 7. Habitat quality and ecological time lag

If Woogaroo's impacted habitat includes mature or complex long-established forest attributes, the key question is whether the offset starts at equivalent quality or has a credible, resourced trajectory to reach it.

The Commonwealth Offset Assessment Guide expressly uses `time until ecological benefit`: how long it takes the offset site to reach its future-with-offset quality. Longer delays generally require a larger offset and increase uncertainty.

Therefore:

```text
future revegetation hectares
  !=
present mature-habitat equivalence
```

unless the quality/time/risk calculation actually supports that equivalence.

### 8. Additionality and existing protection

The offset gain must be additional. Counsel should obtain:

- current tenure;
- existing environmental protections;
- zoning/development constraints;
- any existing conservation covenants;
- existing government/conservation funding;
- baseline probability that the offset vegetation would otherwise be lost;
- the legal instrument proposed to secure the offset.

An area already effectively protected cannot simply be counted again without additional conservation gain.

### 9. Monitoring and success

The public submission questions whether enough baseline MNES surveying exists to establish improvement.

The primary package should therefore identify:

- baseline abundance/use/quality;
- monitoring frequency and method;
- measurable success criteria;
- time horizon;
- confidence assumptions;
- corrective/adaptive actions if targets fail;
- who enforces the obligations;
- whether protection lasts for the life of the impact.

### 10. Local-population and connectivity function

The potentially strongest issue is not generic species substitution but **local functional loss**.

Queensland notes that SEQ koala priority areas are intended to be large, connected areas with the highest likelihood of sustaining koala populations long term, and that core koala habitat modelling uses food/shelter vegetation plus sighting records. Queensland also identifies clearing and fragmentation of eucalypt forest/woodland as drivers of local koala population decline.

If Springview contributes to a connected Ipswich koala population/corridor, the question is whether a distant offset improves or maintains viability **in a way that compensates for that lost local attribute**.

Any genetics argument should still be independently verified before use.

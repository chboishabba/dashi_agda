# Digital Education for Sustainability, and the Sustainability of Digital Education

## Translating Digital-Education Research into Principles for Transformative Education and Sustainable Development

**Manuscript status:** working integrative-conceptual-review draft. A bounded, source-attributed digital-education corpus has been used to generate a provisional set of transferable principles and a sustainability-constraint matrix. The declared Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, ACM Digital Library and IEEE Xplore searches have not yet been executed, so the principles are candidate framework components rather than database-complete review findings. No screening counts or evidence-completeness claims are made here.

## Abstract

Digital transformation and Education for Sustainable Development (ESD) are increasingly treated as connected agendas. Recent scholarship already establishes two important antecedents: digital education can itself generate environmental and social sustainability tensions, and sustainability and digitality can be conceptualised as coupled or twin educational transformations. The present paper therefore does not claim novelty for coupling these agendas. Instead, it asks a more operational question: **what can a mature body of digital-education research legitimately contribute to transformative ESD, under what enactment conditions, and subject to what sustainability constraints?**

We use an integrative conceptual review with a transparent structured-search protocol and a two-corpus synthesis design. First, a bounded corpus of digital-education research is retained source by source and synthesised into seven provisional transferable principles: situated relational engagement; feedback as revisable signal; constitutive learner agency; adaptive support with local choice; plural situated observers; contextual custodianship; and iterative evidence return and recharting. Second, these principles are connected to existing scaling conditions—pedagogy, institutional practice, professional development and policy—and cross-pollinated with independently sourced environmental, social, economic, epistemic, infrastructural, lifecycle and longitudinal sustainability constraints. A separate externality-incidence audit prevents total impact from being treated as sufficient information about who contributed, who benefits, who bears burden, who has voice, who controls or mediates the system, who can exit, and where and when burdens arise. Source roles remain separate throughout: pedagogical evidence does not create lifecycle evidence; institutional guidance does not create intervention effects; cross-domain calibration does not create political conclusions; and cross-paper synthesis is not attributed back to individual source papers as an empirical finding.

The framework is deliberately provisional. Candidate principle generation may precede search closure, but final promotion requires the principles to be challenged and revised against the searched, screened and structured-extracted corpus. The paper's candidate contribution is therefore an **evidence-governed transfer architecture** for moving from established digital-education research toward transformative ESD while distinguishing technology presence, implementation activity, institutional integration, task performance, learning, durability, system transformation and distributional incidence.

## 1. Introduction

Education systems are being asked to respond simultaneously to two large transitions: the transition toward more sustainable societies and the rapid diffusion of digital and artificial-intelligence technologies. UNESCO's ESD for 2030 roadmap places transformation at the centre of Education for Sustainable Development, with priority action areas spanning policy, learning environments, educator capacity, youth and local action. The framework therefore concerns changes in educational systems and practices rather than the addition of sustainability content to otherwise unchanged instruction.

This system-level distinction matters empirically. UNESCO's 2026 mid-term evaluation of ESD for 2030 reports substantial implementation activity while also identifying limited systemic transformation and continuing needs for strategic coherence, monitoring and national ownership. The coexistence of activity with limited transformation motivates a central distinction in this review: implementation activity, technology uptake and programme visibility cannot be treated as sufficient evidence of educational-system transformation.

A parallel distinction is prominent in digital education. The OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 distinguishes successful task performance with generative AI from learning and emphasises pedagogical intent, human-centred teaching, research, governance and enabling infrastructure. Martínez García and colleagues' 2026 systematic review of compulsory-school digital education similarly reports that digital education is often conflated with ICT use or digital competence and that positive educational outcomes depend on pedagogical purpose, teacher mediation, coherent policy and infrastructure, and equitable access rather than device presence alone. These findings reinforce a foundational premise of the present paper: a technology can be present without supplying the educational mechanism, institutional capacity or transformation that a downstream sustainability claim requires.

The relationship between sustainability and digital education is likewise no longer conceptually unexplored. Chugh's 2026 sustainability-paradox paper explicitly frames educational digitalisation as capable of improving access while creating environmental and social costs, with implications for lifecycle thinking, procurement and circularity. Böhme's 2026 digitainability framework goes further by conceptualising sustainability and digitality as a coupled twin transformation. These close antecedents materially narrow the novelty claim of the present review. We do **not** claim that digital education must itself be sustainable as a new proposition, nor that connecting digitality and sustainability is novel.

The research opportunity is instead translational and evidential. A mature digital-education research literature already contains findings and conceptual tools concerning online engagement, feedback, learning support, student voice, pedagogical design, interpretation, context, participation and institutional relations. The Special Issue problem can therefore be reframed as follows:

\[
\text{established digital-education insights}
\xrightarrow{\text{source-attributed synthesis}}
\text{candidate transformative principles}
\xrightarrow{\text{sustainability constraints}}
\text{digital-ESD framework}.
\]

The transfer is not automatic. A finding about online engagement does not become evidence of sustainable development merely because the destination problem concerns ESD. A student-feedback classifier does not become student meaning. A student-identified support strategy does not become a universal intervention. Likewise, sustainability sources do not retroactively become findings of the digital-education papers from which the pedagogical principles were derived.

This separation is especially important because primary institutional evidence suggests that the two agendas are advancing at different speeds. The UNECE Fifth Evaluation Report of the Strategy for ESD synthesises 31 national reports from the 2021–2025 implementation phase and identifies continued expansion of digital access, tools and resources alongside limited and uneven intentional integration of sustainability principles into digital-education policy and practice. Thus:

\[
\text{digitalisation progressing}
+
\text{ESD progressing}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{digital-ESD integration}.
\]

The reverse sustainability direction is also increasingly concrete. The 2026 UNESCO–UNICEF–ITU Charter for Public Digital Learning Platforms treats public digital learning platforms as education-system infrastructure and identifies public accountability, inclusion, pedagogical purpose, openness, interoperability and trustworthiness as design and governance principles. In September 2026, education ministers and designated representatives convened by UNESCO adopted a statement framing education as a human right and common good in the age of AI and calling for deliberative governance, public accountability and protection of learner and teacher rights. Associated UNESCO consultation materials also foreground AI procurement and total cost of ownership. These are normative and governance sources; they do not prove that a named intervention is effective or sustainable.

In parallel, ITU-T L.1410 provides methodology for life-cycle assessment of ICT goods, networks and services, while ITU-T L.1023 provides a circularity scoring method covering durability, repair, reuse, recycling and upgradeability. These sources allow sustainability questions to be expressed in explicit measurement and governance coordinates rather than by calling digital education generically 'green'.

The resulting framework remains reciprocal:

\[
\text{Digital education} \rightleftarrows \text{Education for Sustainable Development}.
\]

But reciprocity is now only the outer structure. The paper's generative centre is the **evidence-governed translation of digital-education expertise into candidate transformative principles**, followed by independent scaling, sustainability and incidence tests. Conceptually:

\[
\text{TransformativeDigitalESD}
=
\text{credible pedagogical mechanism}
\times
\text{enactment capacity}
\times
\text{sustainability admissibility}
\times
\text{distributional incidence adequacy}
\times
\text{scope-appropriate evidence}.
\]

This is a conceptual decomposition rather than an empirical effect equation. Its purpose is to stop one coordinate—for example a short-term learning gain or a total environmental footprint—from silently paying another coordinate such as institutional transformation, participant authority, burden allocation or infrastructure lifecycle sustainability.

## 2. Review aims and research questions

The review has five research questions.

1. **ESD capacity.** Through what pedagogical, curricular, competence, learning-environment and institutional mechanisms can digital education build ESD capacity?
2. **Transformation.** Under what conditions does digital innovation support educational or system transformation rather than technology adoption, implementation activity or task performance alone?
3. **Reflexive sustainability.** How should environmental, social, economic, lifecycle, circularity and infrastructure considerations constrain digital education itself, and how are the resulting benefits and burdens distributed across affected parties, lifecycle stages and time horizons?
4. **Participant governance.** How should learner and participant voice, epistemic agency and governance enter digital-ESD design without treating literature, consent, feedback or consultation as equivalent to local constitutive authority?
5. **Durability and time.** What evidence and conditions bear on durability, institutionalisation, context transfer and longitudinal transformation, and which claims remain future empirical debt?

A cross-cutting methodological question governs all five RQs:

> **Which evidence supports each proposed principle, for whom, over what time horizon, through which observer surface; who contributes, benefits, bears burden, controls or mediates; and what does that evidence not justify?**

## 3. Conceptual framework

### 3.1 From a technology inventory to transferable principles

The starting digital-education corpus is not treated as a list of technologies to be repurposed for sustainability. Its source fibres instead suggest a developmental pattern: identify engagement or feedback signals; restore context hidden by coarse projections; widen participation and epistemic agency; route support through local choice; preserve plural observer perspectives; return evidence; and revise the chart.

From that pattern, seven candidate transferable principles are derived as a **cross-paper synthesis**, not as empirical claims attributed to any one paper.

1. **Situated relational engagement.** Design for meaningful interaction in context rather than access, logins or technology presence alone.
2. **Feedback as revisable signal.** Treat feedback and machine classifications as contestable signals for inquiry rather than semantic or pedagogical authority.
3. **Constitutive learner agency.** Enable learners to shape questions, categories, interpretation, design and evidence return rather than only supply data or receive content.
4. **Adaptive support with local choice.** Route support through context and learner choice rather than fixed category-to-intervention tables.
5. **Plural situated observers.** Retain learner, teacher, family/ally, researcher, institution and model perspectives without treating one projection as the whole educational system.
6. **Contextual custodianship.** Retain person-place context, affordances, effort, value flows and the conditions under which educational data acquire meaning.
7. **Iterative evidence return and recharting.** Treat interventions and interpretations as revisable hypotheses; return evidence, allow contestation and update institutional choices as new information appears.

These principles deliberately preserve the source boundary between the underlying papers and the present synthesis. For example, the 2024 AI-feedback work supplies feasibility evidence for scalable classification, while later voice/agency and ecology-of-data work constrain how such classifications can be interpreted and governed. The resulting 'feedback as revisable signal' principle is therefore a cross-paper inference, not an empirical claim made by all contributing papers.

### 3.2 Enactment and scaling conditions

Transferable principles are not self-executing. The existing digital-ESD transformation model therefore retains four distinct scaling conditions:

- scalable pedagogy;
- institutional practice;
- professional development; and
- policy.

Professional development is consequently treated as an **enactment condition**, not merely another principle. A relational or participatory pedagogy cannot be expected to scale because it has been named; educators require capability, institutions require routines and resources, and policy may either enable or block continued enactment.

The architecture is therefore:

\[
\text{source-attributed principle}
\rightarrow
\text{pedagogy/institution/professional-development/policy conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{sustainability constraints}.
\]

### 3.3 Sustainability as a constraint on digital education

The sustainability corpus is kept independent from the pedagogical corpus. It constrains proposed principles across at least seven dimensions:

- social equity and inclusion;
- epistemic agency and public governance;
- environmental lifecycle and circularity;
- economic total cost of ownership and procurement;
- infrastructure openness, interoperability, portability and exit;
- institutional durability and monitoring; and
- intergenerational option preservation.

This reverse strand intentionally separates:

\[
\text{technology used to teach sustainability}
\neq
\text{sustainable technology}.
\]

It also separates:

\[
\text{pedagogically valuable intervention}
\neq
\text{sustainability-admissible intervention}.
\]

A study may support a learning mechanism while providing no lifecycle evidence about devices, networks, data centres, procurement arrangements or replacement cycles. Equally, an accurate aggregate impact estimate does not by itself identify incidence:

\[
\text{impact magnitude}
\neq
\text{impact incidence}.
\]

The review therefore asks separately who contributed, who benefits, who bears burden, who has voice, who controls or mediates, who can exit, where in the lifecycle an impact occurs, when the burden arrives, and which material position is being observed.

### 3.4 Transformation rather than adoption

The review distinguishes multiple levels that can otherwise be collapsed into a single positive label:

\[
\text{technology presence}
\neq
\text{implementation activity}
\neq
\text{input integration}
\neq
\text{task performance}
\neq
\text{learning/outcomes}
\neq
\text{institutional durability}
\neq
\text{system transformation}.
\]

UNESCO's 2026 mid-term evaluation provides a programme-level illustration of activity without equivalent system transformation. OECD provides the task-performance/learning distinction. Holst and colleagues operationalise the depth and speed of ESD integration across more than 11,000 documents over ten years, while explicitly working at an input-indicator level. Martínez García and colleagues show why digital education cannot be reduced to ICT presence in compulsory schooling. Together these sources motivate a multi-level transformation analysis without making those levels interchangeable.

### 3.5 Epistemic agency and participant governance

Participant engagement is retained as a separate evidence and governance dimension. The digital-education corpus itself supplies an important correction: soliciting feedback is not the same as making students co-producers of inquiry. Students may instead participate in question formation, coding-frame contestation, interpretation, design, choice/rejection of proposed support and evidence-return review.

This distinction carries into ESD. Participatory ESD, consultation and co-design literature can inform a method, but prior participation cannot create authority for a different target population. Context generalisation and same-target-context participant-authority evidence therefore remain independent obligations. Procedural ethics and consent are necessary where applicable but do not substitute for constitutive epistemic agency.

The recurring design questions are:

> **Who participates? Who interprets? Who contributes? Who benefits? Who bears the externalities? Who controls or mediates the infrastructure and decision? Who can contest the categories? Who retains meaningful exit or choice?**

A further distinction—voice present versus testimony uptake versus adequacy of shared interpretive resources—is retained as a candidate future refinement only. It should enter the extraction or audit carrier only if an admitted source/collision shows that the existing participant-authority and voice coordinates are insufficient for the declared consumer.

### 3.6 Contextual custodianship and educational ecology

The ecology-of-data work supplies a particularly productive cross-pollination. Educational data are not interpreted as context-free objects: person and place, available affordances, student effort, institutional conditions and flows of attention, labour, care and value remain material to interpretation.

In digital ESD, that custodianship principle expands beyond data interpretation. Devices, networks, energy, material throughput, repair, procurement, portability, vendor dependence and replacement cycles become part of the educational ecology rather than technical externalities. Externality incidence belongs inside that ecology too: the party carrying a visible burden need not be its sufficient cause, contribution and burden need not coincide, present benefit need not determine later burden, and nominal participation need not identify who controls or mediates the system. These are source-neutral structural constraints; cross-domain industrial and political-economic examples are used only as calibration and do not supply digital-education evidence or political conclusions.

### 3.7 Time, durability and intergenerational option preservation

Short-term outcomes do not determine durable transformation. The review therefore distinguishes immediate performance, learning outcomes, institutionalisation, platform/service persistence, hardware/service-life durability and longer-term system consequences.

Intergenerational framing is used here as an option-preservation constraint rather than as a borrowed cultural-authority claim. A present digital-education choice can be evaluated partly by whether future learners and institutions retain meaningful ability to revise, repair, migrate, exit or reject inherited systems. Openness, portability, interoperability, repair/support windows and procurement lock-in consequently become educational as well as technical concerns. Present benefit does not determine later burden, and present voice does not by itself establish representation of future interests.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Review design

The paper is designed as an **integrative conceptual review with a transparent structured search**. It is not labelled a systematic review and is not an empirical intervention study. The design follows the broad integrative-review logic described by Whittemore and Knafl: problem identification, literature search, data evaluation, data analysis/synthesis and presentation. SANRA is retained as a reporting-quality comparator for narrative review features, not as a universal evidence-ranking instrument. PRISMA-S is used to strengthen reporting of information sources and search execution, and PRESS informs construction and review of database search strategies. Citation of these methods does not itself constitute method execution.

The design has two temporally distinct synthesis phases:

1. **candidate framework generation** from a bounded, source-attributed digital-education corpus plus independently sourced sustainability constraints; and
2. **structured-corpus challenge and revision** after completion of the declared database search, deduplication, screening and extraction.

This distinction allows a conceptual framework to guide review questions without treating the pre-search framework as a review result.

### 4.2 Candidate principle derivation

Candidate principle derivation follows six explicit stages:

1. **Source-bound corpus extraction.** Retain each digital-education paper's source contribution and boundary rather than flattening the corpus.
2. **Cross-paper relation synthesis.** Trace where later papers refine, constrain or extend earlier insights while keeping source claims distinct from cross-paper inference.
3. **Candidate principle derivation.** Form transferable principles only where multiple source fibres support a defensible synthesis.
4. **Scaling-condition attachment.** Identify relevant pedagogy, institutional-practice, professional-development and policy conditions required for enactment.
5. **Sustainability-constraint pairing.** Cross-pollinate each candidate principle with independently sourced environmental, social, economic, epistemic, infrastructure and longitudinal constraints.
6. **Structured-corpus challenge and revision.** Reassess, merge, split, defeat or extend candidate principles against the searched, screened and structured-extracted review corpus.

Candidate principle generation may therefore precede search closure. Final promotion may not:

\[
\text{pre-search candidate framework}
\neq
\text{final review findings}.
\]

### 4.3 Information sources

The declared bibliographic surfaces are:

- Scopus;
- Web of Science;
- ERIC;
- ACM Digital Library; and
- IEEE Xplore.

Open-web searching and source snowballing are used for discovery of primary institutional documents, standards and candidate literature, but they are not substitutes for the declared database searches. Likewise, another systematic review's Scopus or Web of Science search does not pay this manuscript's search execution.

At the date of this working draft, the five database searches, deduplication, eligibility screening and structured extraction remain unexecuted. Their eventual execution records will retain the exact translated query, platform/interface where applicable, date, result count and result export so the lineage can be audited.

### 4.4 Search strategy

The search is organised around six concept families:

1. digital education and ESD;
2. reflexive digital sustainability;
3. lifecycle and circularity;
4. participant agency and governance;
5. longitudinal and institutional impact; and
6. open interoperability and repairability.

A frozen platform-neutral concept vocabulary combines terms such as **digital education**, **educational technology**, **online learning**, **generative AI**, **education for sustainable development**, **sustainability**, **life cycle assessment**, **energy**, **carbon**, **water**, **e-waste**, **circularity**, **repairability**, **interoperability**, **student voice**, **learner agency**, **participatory research**, **co-design**, **longitudinal**, **institutionalisation** and **durability**. Platform-specific syntax, controlled vocabulary, field codes, proximity operators, spelling variants, filters and date restrictions will be translated separately and retained verbatim rather than reconstructed after screening.

### 4.5 Eligibility principles

Because this is an integrative review, admissible evidence can include empirical studies, systematic or scoping reviews, methodological studies, standards, institutional evaluations and policy/framework documents. Inclusion is role-relative rather than source-type-blind. A source must contribute to at least one declared research question and retain enough information to identify its evidence or authority role.

For every included source, the synthesis will retain:

- source identity and source kind;
- publication date;
- population, education level or system context where relevant;
- jurisdiction or institutional context where relevant;
- digital technology or practice under study;
- pedagogy, curriculum or competence mechanism;
- sustainability dimension(s);
- study, review, standard or policy design;
- outcome or claim type;
- time horizon;
- lifecycle/circularity boundary where applicable;
- participant-agency or authority role where applicable;
- interoperability/governance coordinates where applicable;
- **externality incidence**, including contribution, benefit, burden, voice, control/mediation, exit, lifecycle stage, temporal displacement and material position where applicable;
- same-object versus contextual status;
- context-transfer limitations; and
- uncertainty, limitations and defeaters.

Acquisition alone does not create inclusion. Search hits, snippets, standards and institutional reports are not flattened into a single evidence class.

### 4.6 Exclusion and non-promotion rules

Records will be excluded from a research-question synthesis when they do not address the relevant consumer or when the available source does not support the claimed relationship. Several forms of non-promotion are enforced even for retained sources:

- technology presence does not establish educational transformation;
- implementation activity does not establish system transformation;
- input integration does not establish learning or outcome transformation;
- task performance does not establish learning;
- learning gain does not establish system transformation;
- technology used for sustainability education does not establish sustainability of the technology;
- aggregate impact magnitude does not establish contribution, benefit, burden, voice or control distribution;
- a visible burden carrier does not thereby establish sufficient cause;
- present benefit does not establish later burden;
- a lifecycle or circularity method does not create a deployment-specific inventory;
- a standard, charter or ministerial statement does not prove implementation or durability of a deployed system;
- participation literature, consultation or consent does not create local participant authority;
- another review's database execution does not pay this review's execution;
- short-term outcomes do not establish longitudinal durability;
- citation does not import proof or authority;
- cross-paper synthesis is not retroactively attributed to source papers as an empirical finding; and
- source acquisition does not equal manuscript inclusion or evidence-synthesis closure.

### 4.7 Data extraction

Structured extraction will use a **19-coordinate schema** corresponding to the fields above, including an explicit externality-incidence coordinate. The extraction object will retain the included-set identity produced by screening and produce a source-role/scope matrix. Downstream synthesis therefore depends on the exact screened corpus rather than a manually curated bibliography that has lost its search genealogy.

### 4.8 Synthesis and framework challenge

The review will use structured integrative synthesis rather than statistical meta-analysis across heterogeneous source roles. Each synthesis cell is indexed by:

- research question;
- evidence role;
- reciprocity direction;
- transformation status;
- evidence-payment status;
- source reference;
- population/context;
- time horizon; and
- limitation/transfer boundary.

Three broad transformation states are retained—technology adoption only, learning/performance change, and institutional/system transformation—with explicit unresolved status where evidence is insufficient. The wider analysis further separates implementation activity and input integration from those states when sources permit. Externality incidence is retained in structured extraction rather than inferred from aggregate outcomes during synthesis.

The searched corpus will not merely be used to populate a pre-existing seven-box taxonomy. Instead, the seven candidate principles become objects of challenge. A screened source may support a principle, narrow its scope, reveal a missing principle, split a principle into distinct mechanisms, supply a defeater, or show that two principles should be merged. The incidence carrier is equally revisable: a new coordinate such as testimony uptake should be added only if an admitted source and concrete consumer collision show that the current audit loses a decision-relevant distinction. This revision requirement prevents a bounded expert corpus from becoming a closed authority surface.

### 4.9 Lifecycle and circularity interpretation

ICT lifecycle evidence is interpreted against ITU-T L.1410, which separates ICT life-cycle assessment from comparative analysis against a reference product system. Circularity coordinates are informed by ITU-T L.1023, including durability and the ability to repair, reuse, recycle and upgrade. These methods define measurement coordinates; they do not provide an inventory for a digital-education intervention unless same-object deployment data are actually acquired. A process or technology label also does not by itself recover lifecycle stage or quantitative impact.

### 4.10 Participant governance and context transfer

Participant-governance claims are synthesised with a stricter transfer boundary than ordinary literature relevance. Evidence from prior populations can inform candidate mechanisms and study design, but a claim of constitutive epistemic agency in a new target context requires context-generalisation evidence together with participant-authority evidence in that target context. The current conceptual review does not claim to have performed such a local participant-authority study.

### 4.11 Primary institutional and methodological source roles

Primary institutional sources currently retained include UNESCO's ESD for 2030 roadmap and mid-term evaluation; the UNECE Fifth Evaluation Report; OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026; UNESCO's adopted 2026 ministerial statement and separately typed consultation/background materials; the UNESCO–UNICEF–ITU Charter for Public Digital Learning Platforms; and ITU-T L.1410 and L.1023. Each retains its institutional, evaluative, normative or methodological role. None is promoted into an intervention-effect source simply because it is authoritative within its own domain.

Close scholarly antecedents are retained explicitly. Chugh's sustainability-paradox paper pays the conceptual antecedent that digital education itself creates sustainability tensions. Böhme's digitainability framework pays the antecedent that sustainability and digitality can be conceptualised as a coupled twin transformation. Holst and colleagues contribute longitudinal input-monitoring methodology, while Martínez García and colleagues contribute a systematic-review synthesis showing why digital education must be distinguished from ICT presence. These antecedents narrow rather than weaken the present contribution claim.

### 4.12 Methodological status and reproducibility

The formal repository represents search and synthesis as dependent receipts. Database exports feed deduplication; the exact deduplicated object feeds screening; the exact screening object feeds structured extraction; and extraction feeds the source/scope matrix used in synthesis. This prevents downstream claims from being marked complete merely because upstream work is described as completed in prose.

The current draft is therefore reproducible at the level of declared method, source-bounded candidate framework and 19-coordinate extraction schema, but not yet at the level of completed database execution. No unobserved database receipt, screening count, externality-incidence allocation or extracted corpus is reported.

## 5. Candidate transformative-principle matrix

The candidate framework has four layers:

\[
\boxed{
\text{digital-education principle}
\rightarrow
\text{enactment/scaling conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{sustainability constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{externality-incidence audit}
}
\]

The current seven rows are summarised below.

| Candidate principle | Main enactment conditions | Sustainability constraints | Candidate implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situated relational engagement | Scalable pedagogy; institutional practice; professional development | Equity/inclusion; durability/monitoring | Meaningful participation in context matters more than technology presence or access alone. |
| Feedback as revisable signal | Institutional practice; professional development; policy | Epistemic governance; equity; monitoring | Analytics can support inquiry only as contestable, human-reviewed signals. |
| Constitutive learner agency | Scalable pedagogy; institutional practice; policy | Public governance; inclusion; future options | Learners should help shape questions, interpretation and design rather than merely receive content or supply data. |
| Adaptive support with local choice | Scalable pedagogy; institutional practice; professional development | Equity; durability; TCO/procurement | Support should be locally selectable, revisable and institutionally sustainable. |
| Plural situated observers | Institutional practice; professional development; policy | Equity; epistemic governance; burden distribution | Transformation should retain multiple observer perspectives and distinguish beneficiaries, burden bearers, decision controllers and affected voices. |
| Contextual custodianship | Institutional practice; professional development; policy | Lifecycle/circularity; TCO/procurement; openness/interoperability; equity | Educational ecology includes devices, networks, energy, labour, repair, procurement, vendor dependence and the distribution of resulting burdens. |
| Iterative evidence return and recharting | Institutional practice; professional development; policy | Monitoring; public governance; portability/exit; intergenerational options | Institutions should monitor outcomes and incidence, return evidence, revise choices and preserve the possibility of migration or exit. |

The matrix is intentionally not a Cartesian product between every pedagogical idea and every sustainability source. Pairings are retained only where the consumer relationship is meaningful and source roles remain visible. Likewise, the incidence audit does not assign burdens by assumption: any deployment-specific allocation remains an empirical obligation.

## 6. Planned evidence synthesis and revision

The completed evidence synthesis will be organised around five coupled sections:

1. **Digital education as an ESD-capacity mechanism** — pedagogy, curriculum, learning environments, competencies and institutional practice;
2. **From adoption to transformation** — conditions separating tool presence, implementation activity, integration, performance/learning outcomes and system change;
3. **The sustainability of digital education** — lifecycle, circularity, energy/material infrastructure, repairability, interoperability, public digital infrastructure and distributional incidence;
4. **Participant epistemic agency and governance** — voice, co-design, authority, context transfer, institutional choice and control/mediation; and
5. **Durability, institutionalisation and intergenerational consequences** — longitudinal evidence, professional/institutional capacity, support windows, persistence, burden displacement, option preservation and residual empirical debt.

Within each section the final screened corpus will be used to revise rather than merely illustrate the candidate principles.

## 7. Candidate contribution positioning

The present contribution is not the proposition that digital education must itself be sustainable, and it is not the proposition that sustainability and digitality constitute a coupled educational transformation. Those conceptual territories already have close antecedents.

The candidate contribution is narrower:

> **an evidence-governed method for translating established digital-education research into candidate principles for transformative ESD while preserving source provenance, enactment conditions, participant authority, sustainability constraints, externality incidence and unresolved evidence dependencies.**

The approach asks not merely whether a digital practice is innovative or whether it is associated with a positive outcome, but:

- What mechanism is supported?
- Which source and population support it?
- What educator, institutional and policy capability is required for enactment?
- Who participates in interpretation and design?
- Who contributes, who benefits and who bears environmental, social, economic or epistemic burdens?
- Who controls or mediates the relevant infrastructure and decisions?
- Who retains practical refusal, repair, migration or exit options?
- At which lifecycle stage and time horizon do burdens occur?
- What lifecycle and infrastructure commitments are created?
- What evidence remains contextual rather than same-object?
- What can still be repaired, revised, migrated, rejected or exited later?

These questions convert the original digital-education expertise into a testable sustainability-facing framework without claiming that the transfer or any deployment-specific burden allocation has already been empirically validated.

## 8. Current evidence-status boundary

The repository currently contains a bounded digital-education source corpus, source-attributed cross-paper synthesis, a seven-principle candidate framework, a sustainability-constraint matrix, a nine-question externality-incidence audit, a broad open-web/primary-source snowball, lifecycle/circularity methods, participant-governance transfer formalisation, a frozen search protocol and close-antecedent positioning. Those materials are **candidate inputs and pre-search synthesis**, not a completed review corpus.

The following remain operationally unpaid:

- translation of the frozen query families into exact database-specific syntax;
- execution of the five declared bibliographic database searches;
- retained database exports and counts;
- cross-database deduplication;
- eligibility screening with exclusion reasons;
- structured extraction of the included corpus using the 19-coordinate schema;
- empirical allocation of externality incidence for any named deployment;
- challenge/revision of the seven candidate principles and incidence carrier against that corpus; and
- corpus-dependent final evidence synthesis and novelty positioning.

Consequently, this draft does not report review-result counts, pooled effects, prevalence estimates or claims of evidence completeness.
